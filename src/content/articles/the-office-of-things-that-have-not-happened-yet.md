At 9:17 on a Tuesday morning, every mailbox in Bellwether received a letter postmarked tomorrow.

Nobody noticed at first.

The mail carrier, Mr. Vale, was seventy-two and believed strongly in sorting before delivering. He had been known to complain about holiday cards arriving in the wrong season, which he considered a collapse of civilization. On that particular morning he was behind schedule because a truck had stalled on Orchard Road, and so the letters went into the boxes with everything else: electric bills, seed catalogues, coupons for mattresses, a postcard from Florida showing an alligator beside a golf course.

It was only at lunch that people began comparing them.

The first person to understand was Mrs. Ilyan, who owned the bakery.

Her letter was addressed in a handwriting she recognized immediately.

It was hers.

She opened it standing behind the counter, with flour on her wrists.

> Dear Mira,
>
> Don't open the back door at 4:30.
>
> I know you will want to.
>
> Please don't.
>
> —M.

She read it twice.

Then she looked at the clock.

It was 12:06.

The back door led to the alley, where the bakery's rubbish bins stood beneath a rusted fire escape. There was nothing remarkable about it. Mira had opened that door at 4:30 almost every afternoon for twenty-three years.

She put the letter in the till.

At 4:25 she took it out again.

At 4:29 she was standing in front of the door.

At 4:30, she did not open it.

At 4:31, someone knocked.

Mira froze.

Three knocks.

Then two.

Then three again.

She knew the pattern.

It was the knock her husband had used when he came home late.

Her husband had been dead for eleven years.

Mira remained behind the door until the knocking stopped.

The next morning, the alley was empty.

That was when Bellwether began to understand that the letters were not predictions.

They were instructions.

By Wednesday, the town had established three rules.

The first was that every letter concerned its recipient and no one else.

The second was that ignoring a letter did not make it disappear.

The third was that nobody could agree on whether following the letters was better than disobeying them.

Some letters were trivial.

**Don't buy peaches today.**

A woman who ignored that instruction bought six peaches at the supermarket and discovered, when she got home, that every one contained a perfectly formed marble instead of a pit.

A schoolteacher received:

**At 2:14, when Daniel raises his hand, call on Priya instead.**

She did.

Daniel never spoke to her again.

A plumber was told:

**Take the blue ladder.**

He took the red one out of habit.

The blue ladder broke that afternoon.

The red one did not.

There were letters about marriages, accidents, jobs, pregnancies, crimes.

There were letters that said only:

**Stay home.**

And letters that said:

**Go.**

There was one addressed to the mayor that read:

**Do not tell them what happens on Friday.**

Naturally, the mayor told everyone.

This was how Bellwether learned that knowing the future did not make people sensible.

It made them frightened.

On Thursday, the town hall basement filled with residents.

Someone had brought folding chairs. Someone else brought coffee. A woman named Elaine had made a spreadsheet.

The spreadsheet had columns.

**TIME.**

**INSTRUCTION.**

**OUTCOME IF FOLLOWED.**

**OUTCOME IF IGNORED.**

The last column was mostly blank.

"That's the problem," Elaine said.

Nobody asked which problem.

"We don't know what happens if we don't do what we're told."

"Maybe nothing," someone said.

"Maybe everything."

"That's not helpful."

"No," Elaine said. "It isn't."

At the back of the room sat a boy named Jonah, who was twelve.

He had received no letter.

This made him the most important person in Bellwether.

People kept asking him what his letter said.

"Nothing," he would answer.

They thought he was lying.

By Thursday night, people had started leaving food on his family's porch.

The strangest letter arrived at the hospital.

It was given to Dr. Sen, who was forty-six, divorced, and had the unfortunate distinction of being the most trusted person in town.

Her letter contained a single sentence.

**At 8:03 tomorrow morning, a man will come through the emergency entrance carrying a blue suitcase.**

That was all.

At 8:03 on Friday morning, the automatic doors opened.

A man entered carrying a blue suitcase.

Dr. Sen watched from the nurses' station.

The man was perhaps sixty. He wore a gray coat. He looked exhausted.

He placed the suitcase on the floor.

"I need to speak to Dr. Sen," he said.

"I am Dr. Sen."

He looked relieved.

Then he opened the suitcase.

Inside were hundreds of letters.

All of them were addressed to people in Bellwether.

But they were dated for different tomorrows.

"Who are you?" Dr. Sen asked.

The man looked at the letters.

"I don't know."

"Where did you get these?"

"I don't know."

"What's in the suitcase?"

"I don't know."

"Then what do you know?"

The man smiled sadly.

"I know I wasn't supposed to come through the emergency entrance."

Dr. Sen felt something cold move through her.

"What were you supposed to do?"

He reached into his coat and removed a letter.

It was addressed to himself.

He handed it to her.

She opened it.

> Don't let Dr. Sen read this.

For a long moment, neither of them moved.

Then Dr. Sen began to laugh.

She laughed until she cried.

The man laughed too.

It was the first time anyone in Bellwether had laughed since the letters arrived.

---

That afternoon, the mayor ordered the letters collected.

People refused.

The police tried.

People refused more loudly.

Someone proposed burning them.

Someone else proposed locking them in the bank vault.

A lawyer argued that future mail was still mail and therefore protected by federal law.

A theologian suggested that the letters proved nothing except that human beings were very good at misunderstanding signs.

Mira from the bakery stood on the courthouse steps and said:

"We have spent three days trying to obey pieces of paper written by people we haven't become yet."

Everyone went quiet.

She held up her own letter.

"I think that's backwards."

"What do you mean?" someone asked.

Mira looked toward the alley.

"I think the future is asking us questions."

Nobody laughed.

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind we already know how to answer."

---

At 6:00 that evening, Bellwether did something extraordinary.

It stopped.

The shops closed.

The buses stopped.

The hospital postponed everything that could be postponed.

The mayor removed the town clock from the courthouse wall because people had begun watching it obsessively.

Then everyone gathered in the square.

There were perhaps two thousand people.

Everyone carried their letters.

Some held them folded.

Some crumpled them.

Some had memorized them.

A few had burned theirs and carried the ashes.

Jonah stood at the center.

The boy without a letter.

An old woman asked him whether he was frightened.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

He shrugged.

"Nobody told me to be."

---

At 8:03, nothing happened.

At 8:04, nothing happened.

At 8:05, the crowd began to loosen.

Someone laughed.

Someone applauded.

Someone shouted that it was over.

Then Jonah raised his hand.

"Wait."

The town fell silent.

He was looking at the sky.

A small white envelope was descending.

It came down slowly, turning in the air.

Nobody moved.

It landed at Jonah's feet.

His name was written on it.

For the first time, everyone in Bellwether understood what it meant to be afraid of an unopened letter.

Jonah picked it up.

He turned it over.

There was no postmark.

No return address.

He opened it.

Read it.

And smiled.

"What does it say?" the mayor asked.

Jonah folded the letter carefully.

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

He nodded.

"That's all."

He handed it to the mayor.

The mayor read it.

Then she began to cry.

She passed it to Dr. Sen.

Dr. Sen read it.

She passed it to Mira.

Mira read it.

Then she looked at Jonah.

"Is this really all?"

"Yes."

"What does it say?"

Jonah took the letter back.

He read it aloud.

> Dear Jonah,
>
> Whatever you do next is yours.
>
> —Tomorrow

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Then, from somewhere near the fountain, a little girl began to clap.

One person joined her.

Then another.

Soon the whole square was applauding—not because anything had been solved, but because, for the first time in three days, nobody had been told what came next.

The sound traveled down the streets.

Through open windows.

Across rooftops.

Into the alley behind Mira's bakery.

And at exactly 8:17, the back door opened.

Mira was not there.

Nobody was.

The door simply opened in the wind.

Beyond it was the ordinary alley.

A dumpster.

A fire escape.

A puddle reflecting the moon.

Nothing waiting.

Nothing hidden.

Nothing promised.

Just the night.

And, somewhere beyond the reach of any letter, tomorrow.
