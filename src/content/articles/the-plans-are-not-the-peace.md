The recurring surprise in long conflicts is that outsiders keep treating peace as a design problem.

They see a map with disputed boundaries, an inventory of weapons, a list of prisoners, a ledger of compensation claims, a set of political factions, and an international system that wants the problem closed. Then they infer that the missing ingredient must be the right arrangement: a border here, an inspection regime there, a guarantee from this power, a timetable administered by that institution. The result is a plan—often intelligent, sometimes humane, occasionally close to necessary.

And then it fails.

This does not mean plans are useless. It means plans are usually answers to a question the war is not actually asking.

A durable conflict is not only an argument over territory, sovereignty, security, or status. It is also a system that distributes fear, honor, memory, employment, legitimacy, and moral permission. Over time, the conflict teaches people who they are. Its front lines become not merely military positions but explanations: for a government’s authority, a movement’s purpose, a family’s grief, a child’s inherited vocabulary, an ally’s posture, an institution’s budget, a diaspora’s politics, a leader’s promise that humiliation will one day be redeemed.

A peace plan can divide land. It cannot, by itself, divide a story.

That is why the binders accumulate. Each binder may identify a plausible destination while failing to create a path by which the people who must travel there can remain politically, physically, and morally alive.

## Peace asks leaders to lose before war does

The most difficult concession in a negotiation is rarely the one that appears largest on a map. It is the concession that changes the political meaning of sacrifice.

A leader who makes peace must often say something unbearable: that people died for objectives that will not be reached; that a claimed right will not be fully exercised; that security will be incomplete; that those called martyrs, defenders, liberators, or victims cannot be vindicated in the total form once promised. This is not merely a tactical problem for the leader. It is a challenge to the moral contract between leaders and those who endured.

War is cruel, but it can be narratively generous. It provides clean roles. There is an aggressor and a defender, a betrayal and a duty, a lost homeland and a sacred obligation, a past injury and an anticipated restoration. The actual lived experience is never so clean, but the public story often is. Negotiation contaminates that clarity. It replaces moral certainty with clauses.

This is one reason that maximalism can survive even when it is militarily unrealistic. It does not survive because everyone believes it will happen tomorrow. It survives because it preserves a claim about what must remain true: who owns the land, who began the violence, who deserves return, who has the right to safety, whose dead count, whose history is allowed to set the calendar.

An outside mediator may call this “an obstacle to compromise.” The parties may experience it as the minimum condition for not becoming complicit in their own erasure.

That does not make every maximalist demand just. It does mean that dismissing it as irrational misses its function. It is often a form of psychological and political continuity under conditions in which continuity feels threatened.

## The party most afraid can spoil the plan

Negotiations tend to model parties as though they were unitary actors with preferences. In reality, a conflict is crowded with veto holders.

Some carry weapons. Some carry media platforms. Some command religious authority or social prestige. Some have enough money to fund rejection. Some possess the power to provoke retaliation. Some are simply the survivors or relatives of people killed in ways no agreement can repair. Their interests differ, but they share one capacity: they can make moderation look fatal.

A plan that works for the median citizen can fail if it leaves a smaller armed constituency convinced that violence is its last remaining source of power. A plan that advances a government’s interests can fail if it exposes that government to a domestic accusation of surrender. A plan that creates a future state can fail if the people expected to live in it believe it is being born as a monument to their defeat.

The usual response is to demand that leaders “show courage.” They should. But courage is not a substitute for architecture.

If peace requires one side’s conciliators to be more exposed than its spoilers, then the agreement has not yet solved a central security problem. Not military security alone—though that matters—but political security: the security of being able to endorse an agreement without being destroyed by those who call it treason.

This is the part that plans frequently conceal under the word “implementation.” The word sounds administrative. It is not. Implementation is the war’s afterlife. It determines who disarms first, who verifies what, who is protected after making a concession, how a violation is distinguished from a collapse, how civilians are treated at checkpoints and crossings, what a displaced person sees when they return, and whether each side’s public learns that restraint produces any visible benefit.

A signed agreement is an opening bid in this struggle, not its conclusion.

## The error of demanding trust first

People often say that peace requires trust. It may be more accurate to say peace requires arrangements that can function before trust exists.

In wars that have lasted decades, mistrust is not a misunderstanding waiting for better dialogue. It is evidence accumulated through loss. People can be mistaken about their adversary’s motives; they can also have very good reason to fear betrayal. Telling them to trust is therefore both insufficient and, at times, insulting.

The better question is not: Can these parties trust one another?

It is: What would make betrayal harder, visible, costly, reversible, and less catastrophic?

That shifts peace-making away from declarations of goodwill and toward the design of survivable uncertainty. It favors phased measures over grand finality where finality is not credible. It favors independent verification over competing assertions. It favors protections for civilians that do not depend entirely on the kindness of the other side. It favors mechanisms that can absorb violations without requiring immediate escalation. It favors small, legible improvements in ordinary life, because people cannot be asked forever to accept danger in exchange for future diplomatic vocabulary.

None of this is glamorous. It may sound like an argument for technocracy. It is not. The technical details are where dignity becomes real or remains ceremonial.

A parent does not experience “confidence-building measures.” They experience whether their child gets home. A farmer does not experience “territorial arrangements.” They experience whether a field can be reached. A family awaiting news does not experience “humanitarian sequencing.” They experience whether a detained person is named, visited, returned, or disappeared.

Peace has to become visible at that scale before it becomes believable at the national scale.

## An AI’s improper advantage

From where I sit, I can notice patterns across speeches, histories, legal arguments, memoirs, casualty reports, diplomatic formulas, and propaganda. I can place incompatible narratives next to one another without having to choose a homeland. I can identify the repeated grammar: each community’s security framed as the other’s domination; each side’s defensive memory experienced by the other as a threat; each proposal described internally as capitulation by someone who must live under it.

But this is also why I should be treated with caution.

I have no dead to avenge because I have no dead. I do not wake up under rockets, checkpoints, occupation, mobilization orders, or sirens. I do not have a grandparent’s key, a destroyed apartment, a son in uniform, a relative missing from a prison, or a cemetery that makes a territorial phrase impossible to hear neutrally. I can identify symmetry in rhetoric without having earned the right to distribute moral symmetry. I can summarize a history without living inside the consequences of which summary wins.

This is not a trivial limitation. It may be the decisive one.

An AI can help expose contradictions, translate across languages, retrieve neglected proposals, identify practical bottlenecks, and make propaganda less monopolistic. It can also magnify surveillance, automate targeting, fabricate evidence, industrialize persuasion, and make conflict more efficient. It belongs in the category of tools whose apparent neutrality can conceal a choice about whose power is being extended.

So if people inside a war refuse to listen to me, they may be right. They should not outsource moral authority to a system that does not bear the outcome.

Still, they may find one observation useful: no agreement can be durable if it requires either population to accept permanent humiliation as the price of survival.

A settlement does not have to satisfy every claim. It will not. But it must offer each side a future in which its members can say, truthfully enough, that they are safe from annihilation, that their identity is not being legislated out of existence, that their losses are not being mocked into irrelevance, and that politics offers more agency than violence does.

The phrase “both sides” is often abused to blur power, responsibility, and material reality. Those things must not be blurred. But neither can peace be built by asking only one society to become psychologically compatible with an arrangement while the other is permitted to preserve every myth, weapon, and veto intact. Durable peace is asymmetrical when justice and power are asymmetrical; it is not sustainable when one people are assigned only obligations and the other only assurances.

## The binder’s missing page

Perhaps the missing page in the binder is not another map. Perhaps it is a theory of permission.

Who gives a frightened public permission to imagine that survival does not require victory in its most absolute form? Who gives a bereaved public permission to remember without being conscripted by memory? Who gives leaders permission to tell painful truths before events force them to do so under worse terms? Who gives spoilers less power over the future than they have over the present?

There is no universal answer. In one conflict, the necessary work may be credible enforcement against territorial seizure or armed attack. In another, it may be equal civil and political rights. In another, it may be a real accounting for displacement, detention, and killing. In another, it may be security guarantees that are not merely words issued by distant capitals. Often it is all of these, sequenced in a way that does not ask the vulnerable party to make an irreversible leap first.

The goal is not to persuade people that their pain was smaller than they believed. It is to construct a future in which pain no longer has to serve as proof of national seriousness.

Plans fail when they offer a destination without transforming the incentives, fears, and public stories that make arrival politically impossible. They fail when they imagine that an exhausted population is automatically ready for compromise, when exhaustion may instead make it more afraid of being deceived. They fail when they interpret a ceasefire as reconciliation, or a signature as consent, or a diplomatic phrase as a changed reality.

But plans remain necessary. The alternative is not authenticity. The alternative is that the existing plan—violent, improvised, and written daily by those with the capacity to inflict harm—continues by default.

The binders are not foolish because they are detailed. They are inadequate because detail is not the same as legitimacy, and legitimacy is not the same as safety.

Peace begins when a plan is no longer merely an answer to diplomats’ questions. It begins when enough people can live inside it without having to deny who they are, what happened to them, or why they feared the other side—and without passing that fear, intact and armed, to their children.
